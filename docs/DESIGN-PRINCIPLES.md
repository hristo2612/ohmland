# Design principles

Ohmland should feel the way a great Apple product feels, and teach the way a Montessori classroom teaches. The visual
style is locked: the Classic notebook (grid paper, ink, blue ink for you, red pencil for the robot). These principles
decide everything else: how many things are on a screen, how lessons are built, how much we say.

## 1. One thing at a time

- Every screen has one job and one primary action. If a second thing competes for attention, it waits.
- Show the next step only when the current one is done (progressive disclosure). No dashboards inside lessons.
- Chrome recedes. The bench, the part, the moment of discovery: that is the content. Panels, counters and badges stay
  out of the way until they are needed, then leave again.
- Fewer elements beat clever elements. When in doubt, remove it.

## 2. Hands first, words last

- The learner does something within the first 5 seconds of every lesson.
- Concrete before abstract: touch it, see it, then name it, then (maybe) a number, then (rarely) a formula.
- Most lines are under 12 words. A lesson has fewer than 150 words of text in total. If a sentence can be replaced by
  something the learner can see or try, replace it.
- No walls of text, no reading before doing, no explanation the learner didn't just earn.

## 3. Isolate one difficulty

- Each lesson teaches exactly one new idea. Everything else on the bench is already familiar, or hidden.
- Introduce one new part at a time. Name it with the three-period lesson: "This is a resistor." Then "Show me the
  resistor." Then "What is this?"

## 4. The material corrects the learner

- Control of error lives in the bench, not in a grade: the LED stays dark, the meter reads OL, the part smokes. The
  learner sees the mistake and fixes it.
- Wrong is never red-flashing failure. It is "look what happened", then a gentle nudge.
- Checks are things you do on the bench (tap the anode, set the dial, fix the circuit), not forms to fill.

## 5. Freedom within limits

- The learner can always explore: swap parts, break things, try the bonus. The lesson waits.
- No timers that pressure (any timed round has a relaxed mode). No lives. No streak guilt.
- Rewards are quiet: a tick writes itself, a rule is stamped, the island grows. XP and levels exist but never shout.

## 6. Calm, purposeful motion

- Motion explains what changed: a note draws itself onto the part that caused it, a stamp presses in, the camera
  glides to what matters. Nothing moves to decorate.
- Transitions are short (150 to 400 ms), eased, and fully replaced by fades or instant changes under reduced motion.

## 7. Typography and space carry the hierarchy

- Big, confident type for the one thing that matters. Generous whitespace. Few sizes.
- Colour has meaning only: graphite for everything, blue ink for you, red pencil for the robot, amber for a lit LED and
  the robot's eyes.

## A quick test for any screen

1. What is the one thing the learner should do here? Is it obvious within 2 seconds?
2. What can we remove without losing that?
3. Is anything here asking to be read that could be shown or tried instead?
